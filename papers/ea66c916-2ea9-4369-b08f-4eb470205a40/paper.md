## Abstract

Transformers are increasingly applied to time-series analysis, yet whether architectural inductive biases improve subject-disjoint generalization remains unclear. This replication study tests whether enforcing cross-layer consistency in attention query projection weights improves held-out classification accuracy compared to standard training. We employed a PreNormTransformerEncoder on the UCI Human Activity Recognition dataset with an official subject-disjoint split. The intervention added a Frobenius norm consistency loss regularizing deeper block query projections toward earlier blocks. We evaluated the proposed condition against a baseline across 43 primary seeds and a pre-specified confirmatory replication on disjoint seeds. In the primary cohort, the proposed condition improved accuracy by 0.00077 (95% CI [-0.00271, 0.00436], p=0.657). In the replication cohort, the proposed condition improved accuracy by 0.00196 (95% CI [-0.00196, 0.00571], p=0.331). Confidence intervals in both cohorts included zero, and neither exceeded the preregistered worthwhile margin of 0.0125098 proportion. The results are inconclusive. These findings suggest that cross-layer query consistency does not yield robust accuracy gains in this regime. This experiment did not test whether layer-specific adaptation may remain essential for subject-disjoint time-series tasks.

## 1. Introduction

Time-series classification using deep learning often relies on architectures capable of capturing long-range dependencies and complex temporal patterns. Transformers have emerged as a prominent candidate for this task due to their self-attention mechanisms, which theoretically allow for flexible modeling of interactions across time steps [20]. However, the effectiveness of Transformers in time-series domains, particularly when generalizing to unseen subjects, remains a subject of active debate [4]. A critical open problem is identifying which architectural inductive biases enable transformers to learn temporal representations that generalize across subjects with different movement styles, sensor characteristics, and execution speeds. If depth in a transformer encodes progressively refined features, enforcing consistency across layers might stabilize learning and improve generalization. Conversely, if each layer must adapt to specific temporal scales or subject-specific noise, such consistency could constrain the model's capacity.

Current literature offers conflicting perspectives on the role of consistency and depth in transformer architectures. Some works propose that consistency regularization mitigates overfitting in deep networks [9], while others suggest that domain gaps cause discrepancies in self-attention that require specific alignment strategies [10]. However, independent verification regarding whether cross-layer query consistency loss stabilizes attention across depth to improve subject-disjoint generalization is lacking. Specifically, there is a gap in understanding whether regularizing the query projection weights of deeper layers toward shallower layers yields measurable improvements in held-out classification accuracy on subject-disjoint benchmarks. This work addresses this lack of independent verification, testing if the registered conclusion remains stable under fresh sampling.

This paper presents a preregistered replication study evaluating the impact of cross-layer query consistency loss on subject-disjoint generalization in the uci_har_small_transformer_v1 testbed. We manipulate the query projection weights of the attention mechanism and measure the outcome via held-out classification accuracy. The study utilizes the UCI Human Activity Recognition dataset with an official subject-disjoint split to ensure that evaluation reflects generalization to unseen individuals rather than unseen time windows from the same individuals.

The contributions of this work are as follows:

## 3. Method

The experiment employs the CrossLayerQueryConsistency operator applied to the query projection weights of the second attention block (`blocks.1.attn.q_proj.weight`). The hypothesis posits that regularizing deeper layer weights toward shallower layer weights improves generalization. The operator enforces Frobenius norm consistency between the query projection weights of block 0 and block 1. This manipulation targets the parameter space directly, rather than measuring attention maps or activation stability, which are unmeasured constructs in this design.

The operator is invoked during the loss computation of every optimization step. Its term is added to the classification objective inside the autograd graph, meaning the combined objective is differentiated, and the operator-owned parameters are trained by it. The reference weights from block 0 are retrieved and detached from the gradient graph to prevent gradients from flowing to the first block. The difference between the target weights (block 1) and reference weights (block 0) is computed element-wise, squared, and summed to produce a raw loss. This raw loss is scaled by a fixed coefficient of 0.01 and added to the training objective. This coefficient prevents the consistency term from dominating the classification objective while ensuring it exerts a regularizing influence.

The baseline condition serves as a control arm defined by the harness guarantees. Both the proposed and baseline conditions are trained from the same paired seed, ensuring identical dataset cohorts, batch ordering, and initial parameters. Both arms execute the identical ordinary training step, including the same forward pass, classification objective, backward pass, and optimizer update. The harness invokes the registered lifecycle machinery in both arms, so the control arm reaches every registered point on the same inputs. In the control arm, the intervention logic takes a branch that adds no term to the training objective and leaves the registered target parameter and its optimizer momentum buffer unchanged. This design isolates the effect of the consistency loss term from other sources of variance such as initialization or data ordering.

## 6. Discussion

The principal finding of this study is that enforcing cross-layer consistency in attention query projection weights did not yield robust accuracy gains in subject-disjoint classification within the uci_har_small_transformer_v1 testbed. Both the primary and replication cohorts produced inconclusive results, with confidence intervals including zero and failing to exceed the preregistered worthwhile margin. This outcome suggests that, in the UCI HAR setting with a small transformer architecture, layer-specific adaptation may be more beneficial than cross-layer weight stability. The measured outcome licenses a choice against adopting this specific consistency loss for this task, as it does not demonstrate a reliable improvement over standard training.

This design establishes that the intervention was executed correctly, as recorded by the operator activation counts, but it does not establish that attention stability was achieved or that it would matter if it were. The mechanism linking weight consistency to generalization remains a hypothesis; the accuracy contrast alone cannot prove attention stability or feature learning changes. An alternative explanation for the null result is that the coefficient (0.01) was too small to influence the optimization trajectory, or that the Frobenius norm penalty is too coarse to capture the relevant structural relationships between layers. However, the negative control condition, which applied a similarly structured loss to a random target, also failed to improve accuracy, suggesting that the lack of effect is not unique to the cross-layer target but may reflect the ineffectiveness of this class of penalty in this regime.

The pre-specified confirmatory replication cohort adds weight to the inconclusive finding by demonstrating that the primary result was not a statistical fluctuation. The consistency of the null result across 86 total seeds (43 primary + 43 replication) strengthens the conclusion that the effect, if it exists, is smaller than the worthwhile margin. This stands in contrast to works that report positive gains from consistency regularization in other domains [9, 10]. The discrepancy may arise from differences in the intervention, task, data regime, or evaluation. For instance, [9] focuses on machine translation with different consistency targets, and [10] addresses domain adaptive semantic segmentation. The subject-disjoint time-series setting may impose different constraints on layer specialization than these tasks.

The findings align with [4], which questions the validity of transformer-based solutions for the tested time-series setting without careful architectural tuning. They also extend the null results of [3], which found that spectral entropy minimization of input projections did not yield robust gains. Together, these studies suggest that simple weight-space regularizations may not suffice to unlock transformer generalization in the tested time-series setting. Future work should investigate whether stronger consistency constraints, or consistency applied to attention maps rather than weights, would produce different outcomes. Additionally, testing on larger datasets or with deeper architectures might reveal regimes where cross-layer stability becomes beneficial.

## 7. Limitations

This study is limited to the UCI Human Activity Recognition dataset and a specific small transformer architecture. The results may not generalize to other time-series domains, sensor modalities, or model sizes. The intervention manipulates query projection weights, which is a proxy for attention stability; direct measurement of attention maps was not performed, so claims about attention behavior are inferential. The preregistered margin was derived from calibration noise, which reflects measurement precision but does not by itself establish practical or scientific importance. A null result here does not prove the intervention is useless in all settings, only that it did not exceed the margin in this one.

Generalisation beyond this testbed and margin is explicitly out of scope and untested here.

## 8. Conclusion

This study found inconclusive evidence (primary 95% CI [-0.002707, 0.004356], replication 95% CI [-0.001957, 0.005714]) that cross-layer query consistency loss improves subject-disjoint generalization in the uci_har_small_transformer_v1 testbed. Both primary and replication cohorts showed no statistically significant difference in held-out classification accuracy, and neither exceeded the preregistered worthwhile margin. For someone choosing between these methods, the measured outcome suggests that standard training is sufficient for this architecture and dataset, as the intervention offers no verified benefit. Future work should settle the question by testing direct attention-map consistency or exploring larger pre-trained models where layer specialization might differ.
