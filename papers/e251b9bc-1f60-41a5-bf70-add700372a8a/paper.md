## Abstract

Dynamic regularization based on optimizer state geometry is proposed as an alternative to static weight regularizers, yielding lower held-out classification accuracy in small-data transformers. This study tests whether Momentum-Orthogonal Projection (MOP), which orthogonalizes target weights to the optimizer's momentum buffer, improves subject-disjoint generalization on time-series data. We preregistered a hypothesis that the intervention would yield higher held-out classification accuracy than the comparator, exceeding a fixed worthwhile margin of 0.0125098 proportion. We evaluated the proposed condition against a baseline and a negative control using a PreNormTransformerEncoder on the UCI Human Activity Recognition dataset with an official subject-disjoint split. The experiment employed 43 primary seeds and 43 disjoint confirmatory-replication seeds. In the primary cohort, the proposed condition worsened accuracy by 0.01171 versus the baseline (95% CI [-0.01744, -0.00664], p=0.0002). In the replication cohort, the proposed condition worsened accuracy by 0.01757 versus the baseline (95% CI [-0.0218, -0.0130], p=0.0002). Both confidence intervals excluded zero and lay entirely below the worthwhile margin. The preregistered decision was that the direction was refuted in both cohorts. These measured outcomes indicate that projecting weights orthogonal to the momentum buffer does not improve subject-disjoint generalization in this regime and may degrade performance.

## 1. Introduction

Deep learning models for time-series analysis frequently operate in data-constrained regimes where generalization to unseen subjects is critical. In edge-AI applications, such as human activity recognition on wearable devices, models must perform robustly on individuals not represented in the training set. Standard regularization techniques, such as weight decay or dropout, often fail to capture the specific geometric properties of the optimization trajectory that might influence cross-subject generalization. While momentum-based optimizers are ubiquitous, their internal state—specifically the momentum buffer—accumulates gradient history that reflects the optimization landscape. Theoretical work suggests momentum induces implicit regularization through time-delayed parameter updates, and recent optimizer designs explicitly orthogonalize momentum matrices to exploit input-output geometries. However, it remains unsettled whether dynamically modifying weights to be orthogonal to this accumulated momentum buffer enhances generalization where static regularizers fail.

Current literature on optimizer geometry has established methods that modify momentum structures to address optimization challenges. Orthogonalized momentum motivates matrix-aware methods such as Muon that orthogonalize momentum matrices before parameter updates to exploit input-output geometry [1]. Other studies examine how momentum affects the generalization performance of optimization algorithms through implicit regularization [8], or manipulate stochastic gradient noise as implicit regularization important for generalization [7].

In the context of time-series and subject-disjoint settings, existing literature indicates tests of architectural or weight-based regularizers rather than optimizer-state geometry. Surveys highlight challenges posed by distribution shifts and non-stationary dynamics in time series [5]. Previous preregistered tests on the same testbed evaluated cross-layer consistency losses [2, 4] and spectral entropy minimization of input projections [10], all carrying erratum notices, finding inconclusive results regarding subject-disjoint accuracy. Row-norm variance regularization of feed-forward weights also yielded inconclusive outcomes in this regime [3], which carries an erratum notice. While momentum orthogonalization improves optimization stability and time-series regularization addresses subject-disjoint accuracy, no prior work tests whether projecting weights orthogonal to the optimizer's momentum buffer specifically enhances subject-disjoint generalization on small time-series data.

This paper presents a preregistered experiment testing whether Momentum-Orthogonal Projection improves held-out classification accuracy on unseen subjects. ## 3. Method

The core of this study is the Momentum-Orthogonal Projection (MOP) operator, applied as a post-step modification to the target parameter tensor. The hypothesis posits that breaking alignment between the weights and the accumulated gradient history (stored in the momentum buffer) may improve generalization. The operator is applied only in the intervention arm; in the control arm, it does nothing. Both arms share identical initialization, data ordering, hyperparameters, and training loop, differing only in this single post-step modification.

The operator modifies the target parameter tensor W by subtracting its projection onto the optimizer's momentum buffer M, row-wise. This ensures W becomes orthogonal to M on rows where M has non-negligible norm. The specification below is the preregistered operator contract, fixed before any compute was spent. The scope is row-wise, applied after the optimizer step of every optimization step. The ordering is parallel across rows. Numerical safeguards include an epsilon of 1e-12 on the denominator and skipping rows if the M norm is zero.

The algorithm proceeds as follows: receive target parameter tensor W, optimizer momentum buffer M, and condition flag. If the condition flag equals 'control', terminate without modifying W. Compute row-wise squared norms of M. Create a mask for valid rows where the norm exceeds 1e-12. If no valid rows exist, terminate. Compute row-wise dot products between W and M. Compute projection scalars alpha as the dot product divided by the norm squared plus epsilon. Broadcast alpha to match W shape. Compute the projection update delta as alpha expanded times M. Apply the orthogonal projection by subtracting delta from W on valid rows in-place. Return. The following invariants hold throughout: W row norm decreases or stays same, and W becomes orthogonal to M on valid rows.

## 6. Discussion

The principal finding is that Momentum-Orthogonal Projection worsened held-out classification accuracy in both the primary and replication cohorts. The measured outcome indicates that projecting weights orthogonal to the optimizer's momentum buffer does not improve subject-disjoint generalization in this regime. The effect was statistically significant and negative, with mean differences of -0.01171 and -0.01757 respectively. Both confidence intervals excluded zero and lay entirely below the preregistered worthwhile margin. This result refutes the preregistered direction hypothesis.

What this means for someone choosing between these methods is that the proposed condition should not be adopted for improving subject-disjoint accuracy on the uci_har_small_transformer_v1 testbed. The measured outcome licenses a decision to prefer the baseline over the intervention in this specific setting. It does not establish that optimizer-state geometry is irrelevant for generalization in all settings, but it indicates that this specific operator implementation does not yield benefits here.

A mechanism that would produce the observed result is that orthogonalizing weights to the momentum buffer disrupts the implicit regularization provided by momentum accumulation. Momentum is interpreted as solving an l2-regularized minimization problem [6], and disrupting this relationship might remove a beneficial bias. An alternative mechanism is that the projection introduces noise or instability in the parameter updates that harms convergence on small datasets. These mechanistic rationales are hypotheses, not measured findings; the experiment measured only accuracy.

The pre-specified confirmatory replication cohort adds confidence that the negative result is not due to seed-specific variance. The held-out classification accuracy was lower in the proposed cohort (mean 0.8951) than the baseline cohort (mean 0.9068), suggesting the degradation is robust across seeds. Unlike cross-layer consistency losses that showed no significant effect [2, 4], this intervention showed a significant negative effect. This suggests that dynamic parameter modification post-step may be more disruptive than adding consistency losses to the objective.

The finding stands against specific cited papers regarding momentum orthogonalization. While orthogonalized momentum motivates matrix-aware methods such as Muon for optimization stability [1], this study shows it translates to lower subject-disjoint accuracy (mean 0.8951 vs 0.9068) in this context. Memory-efficient subspace optimization investigates strategies [9], but our results suggest these strategies do not improve generalization. Theoretical work linking momentum to implicit regularization [6, 8] is consistent with the hypothesis that disrupting momentum alignment affects generalization, but the direction observed was negative rather than positive.

What would settle the question next is testing whether different projection targets (e.g., orthogonalizing to the gradient rather than momentum) or different timing (e.g., pre-step rather than post-step) yield different outcomes. Additionally, testing on larger datasets would determine if the negative effect is specific to the uci_har_small_transformer_v1 testbed.

## 7. Limitations

This design cannot tell anyone whether optimizer-state geometry affects generalization in larger datasets or different modalities. The subject-disjoint split tests cross-subject generalization on the UCI HAR dataset; it is not evidence about a different corpus, sensor, task or deployment distribution. The negative control tests whether projection mechanics alone affect accuracy, but it does not isolate the specific geometric property of orthogonality to momentum. Mechanistic interpretations remain hypotheses because only accuracy was measured; attention stability, feature learning, or layer specialization were not directly recorded. The margin is a calibration-derived threshold and does not by itself establish practical or scientific importance. The confirmatory cohort is a replication on new seeds within the same held-out split, not an independent replication on new subjects or datasets.

Declared scope. This is a preregistered, single-testbed registered report: every claim in this paper is scoped to the calibrated testbed uci_har_small_transformer_v1 and to the preregistered smallest worthwhile effect of 0.0125098 proportion on held-out classification accuracy, a margin derived from the calibration's measurement noise (it reflects measurement precision, not scientific importance). Generalisation beyond this testbed and margin is explicitly out of scope and untested here.

## 8. Conclusion

This study found that Momentum-Orthogonal Projection worsened held-out classification accuracy compared to the baseline in both primary and replication cohorts. The measured outcome indicates that this intervention does not improve subject-disjoint generalization on the uci_har_small_transformer_v1 testbed. The preregistered direction was refuted. For someone choosing between these methods, the results license preferring the baseline. The next step to settle the question would be testing alternative projection targets or timing, and evaluating on larger datasets to determine if the negative effect is specific to this data regime.
